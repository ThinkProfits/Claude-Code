# Should You Sell Your Saskatchewan Farmland as a Unit or in Pieces?

> **Quick Answer:** Selling your Saskatchewan farmland as a unit is usually simpler and suits buyers who want a whole operation, while selling in pieces (by quarter or by block) can widen the buyer pool and capture premiums on your best parcels. Which one gets you more depends on how much your soil varies from quarter to quarter, who is buying in your area and how much time you have.

When you've spent decades putting a farm together a quarter at a time, it can feel natural to sell it the same way it was built, or to sell it all at once and be done. Both instincts make sense. For most Saskatchewan landowners, deciding whether to sell farmland as one unit or in pieces is the biggest choice of the whole sale, and it often matters more than the asking price. The right answer depends on your land, your local buyers and your own plans. Grant Ostapowich has farmed for more than 30 years and has sold farmland across Saskatchewan for 17, and this is one of the first conversations he has with almost every seller. Here's how he helps landowners think it through.

## What Selling as a Unit vs in Pieces Actually Means

Selling as a unit means one buyer takes all of your land in a single deal, while selling in pieces means separate buyers for separate parcels.

A **unit sale** packages every quarter, and often the yard site, bins and other improvements, into one transaction. There's one buyer, one set of conditions and one closing date. A **piecemeal sale** splits the land into individual quarters or blocks of quarters, each sold on its own terms, sometimes to neighbours who already farm next to a specific parcel.

There's also a middle path. Some sellers invite offers on individual parcels and on the whole package at the same time, then compare the total. That lets the market show you which approach pays more instead of guessing up front.

## When Selling as a Unit Makes Sense

A unit sale tends to work best when the land is consistent, connected and attractive to larger buyers.

- **The land is contiguous and fairly even in quality.** If your quarters touch each other and the soil is similar across the block, a buyer can farm it as one efficient operation, and there's little to gain from splitting it.
- **The yard site and infrastructure tie the farm together.** Bins, a shop and a central yard are worth the most to someone farming the surrounding land. Splitting the quarters away from the yard can leave those improvements hard to value on their own.
- **Your likely buyer is a large operator or investor.** Some buyers are specifically looking to add a large block in one step and will pay for the convenience of doing it in a single deal.
- **You want one clean closing.** One buyer means one set of legal work, one possession date and less time spent managing the sale. That can matter a great deal when an estate is involved or you've already set a retirement date.

## When Selling in Pieces Can Get You More

Selling in pieces can raise your total when different parcels appeal to different buyers.

- **Neighbours will often pay more for land next to theirs.** A quarter that borders an active operation can be worth more to that farmer than to anyone else, because it fits their machinery routes and management. Selling that quarter separately lets that buyer compete for it directly.
- **Your quarters vary a lot in quality.** In a unit sale, a buyer tends to price the whole package with the weaker land in mind. Selling your strongest quarters on their own lets each one earn its own price, which is why soil variability is often the deciding factor.
- **Smaller buyers can only afford one or two quarters.** Many young farmers and smaller operations can't finance a whole farm but can stretch for a single quarter. Splitting the land brings them into the buyer pool.
- **You have different land types.** Cropland, pasture and hunting or recreational land attract different buyers. Grassland in particular has its own market, as we explain in our look at [pastureland values in Saskatchewan](https://mysaskfarm.com/the-grass-ceiling-why-pastureland-remains-the-ranchers-stronghold/).

Soil variability is easy to underestimate. Even SAMA's [assessment methods](https://www.sama.sk.ca/property-owner-services/understanding-assessment/methods-assessment) rate cultivated land quarter by quarter on its soil and productive capacity, which is a good reminder that two parcels on the same farm can be very different assets. For a deeper look at what drives per-acre value, see our guide to [how much an acre of farmland is worth in Saskatchewan](https://mysaskfarm.com/acre-farmland-worth-saskatchewan/).

## The Trade-Offs to Weigh Before You Decide

Every option has costs that don't show up in the headline price per acre.

- **Leftover parcels can sit.** If your best quarters sell quickly, the weaker ones may take longer and sell for less, which can pull down the total you were counting on. It's worth having a plan for the last parcel before you list the first.
- **More deals mean more paperwork.** Several buyers can mean several sets of conditions, financing timelines and closings. Your legal costs and the time you spend managing the sale both go up.
- **Leases and renters need to be dealt with.** If some of your land is rented out, the timing and terms of those agreements can affect which parcels are attractive and when they can be handed over. Check your lease terms before you decide how to split the sale.
- **Taxes may land differently.** Selling over several months or across two calendar years can change how and when a capital gain is reported. ## How Grant Helps Sellers Decide

The best structure usually becomes clear once the land, the buyers and your goals are all on the table.

1. **Review the land quarter by quarter.** Grant looks at the legal descriptions, cultivated acres, soil and any improvements for each parcel. This shows quickly whether the farm is consistent enough to sell as one block or varied enough that splitting it could pay.
2. **Map the likely buyers.** Next comes who is actually active in your area: neighbours expanding, larger operators, investors or buyers looking for grass or hunting land. Knowing who is out there is often what decides the question.
3. **Talk through your timeline and goals.** A seller who needs one clean closing before a set date has different priorities from one who can wait for the best price on each parcel. Grant will ask what matters most to you and your family before recommending anything.
4. **Test the market when it makes sense.** Where the answer isn't obvious, a mixed approach that invites offers both ways lets buyers show you which option pays more. You stay in control of which offers to accept.

Timing also plays a part. Most farmland in Saskatchewan changes hands in the winter months, so if you're weighing your options now, our guide to the [best time to sell farmland in Saskatchewan](https://mysaskfarm.com/best-time-sell-farmland-saskatchewan/) explains how to prepare.

## Make the Decision With Your Land and Buyers in View

There's no single right answer to selling farmland as a unit or in pieces. A unit sale offers simplicity and suits contiguous, consistent land and larger buyers. Selling in pieces can reward you when your quarters vary, when neighbours are keen or when smaller buyers are active. Either way, the decision deserves a look at your actual land and your local market before the first listing goes up. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it better to sell farmland as one unit or by the quarter?

It depends on the land and the buyers. A unit sale usually suits contiguous land of similar quality with a yard site that ties it together, while selling by the quarter can pay more when soil quality varies or neighbours want specific parcels. A mixed approach, inviting offers on individual quarters and on the whole package, lets the market answer the question.

### Will I get more per acre if I sell my quarters separately?

Sometimes, but not always. Your best quarters may earn a higher price per acre on their own, especially from a neighbouring buyer, but weaker parcels can sell for less and take longer. Compare the total you'd expect from all the pieces, minus the extra time and legal costs, against a realistic offer for the whole unit.

### Can I sell my land but keep the yard site?

Often, yes, but carving a yard site out of a quarter usually requires subdivision approval, which takes time. Check with your RM office early about what's involved in your area. Keeping the yard can also affect what buyers will pay for the remaining land, so factor that into your plan.

### How long does it take to sell farmland in pieces?

Expect a piecemeal sale to take longer than a single unit sale, because each parcel needs its own buyer, conditions and closing. ### Does selling in pieces change my taxes?

It can. Spreading sales over several months or across two tax years can change when capital gains are reported and how exemptions apply. This is general information, not tax advice, so talk to your accountant before you decide on a structure.
