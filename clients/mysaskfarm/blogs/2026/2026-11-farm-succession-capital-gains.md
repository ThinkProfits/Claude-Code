# Farm Succession and Capital Gains in Saskatchewan: What Farmland Owners Need to Know Before Selling

> **Quick Answer:** When you sell Saskatchewan farmland, CRA generally taxes one-half of your capital gain, but land that meets the rules for qualified farm or fishing property may be eligible for the lifetime capital gains exemption ($1.25 million for 2025). Farmland passed to a child, during your life or at death, can often move on a tax-deferred basis instead. Whether you can reduce or defer capital gains on farmland, including inherited farmland, depends on conditions your accountant needs to check before you list.

> **Please note:** This article is general information to help you prepare for conversations with your advisors. It is not tax, legal or financial advice. Tax rules change and every family's situation is different, so talk to your accountant and lawyer before you make any decision about selling or transferring farmland.

## How Capital Gains Work When You Sell Farmland

A capital gain is the difference between what you sell the land for and what it cost you, after allowable adjustments.

Your starting point is the land's adjusted cost base (ACB), which is generally what you paid plus certain costs of buying and improving it. Subtract that and your selling costs (such as commission and legal fees) from the sale price, and what's left is your capital gain. For 2025, CRA says your taxable capital gain is generally one-half of your capital gain, and that taxable portion is added to your income for the year. A proposed increase to that rate was cancelled in 2025, so the one-half rate still applies.

For land held for decades, the gain can be large. Statistics Canada's average value of Saskatchewan farmland and buildings rose from $1,798 per acre in 2021 to $2,943 in 2025 (see [how much an acre of farmland is worth in Saskatchewan](https://mysaskfarm.com/acre-farmland-worth-saskatchewan/)), so finding your original cost records early matters.

## What Counts as Qualified Farm Property

Qualified farm or fishing property (QFFP) is the label that can make farmland eligible for the lifetime capital gains exemption.

CRA's farming guide, [RC4060, Chapter 6](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/forms-publications/publications/rc4060/rc4060-09.html), sets out the conditions. In broad strokes:

- **Ownership period.** The land generally has to have been owned by you, your spouse or common-law partner, your children or parents, or a qualifying family farm partnership for at least 24 months before the sale.
- **Use in farming.** The land has to have been used mainly in a farming business in Canada, with you or a qualifying family member actively engaged in that business on a regular and ongoing basis.
- **An income test for some property.** For some land, CRA also looks at whether gross income from the farming business was greater than income from other sources for at least two years of ownership. Your accountant will know which tests apply to your land.

Rented land is the tricky part for many Saskatchewan owners. If you've rented your quarters to a neighbour for years, whether the land still qualifies depends on when you acquired it and how it has been used, and your accountant will check that first.

## The Lifetime Capital Gains Exemption for Farmland

If your land qualifies, the lifetime capital gains exemption (LCGE) can shelter a large part of the gain.

According to CRA, the LCGE for qualified farm or fishing property is $1,250,000 for 2025, which works out to a maximum capital gains deduction of $625,000 (one-half of the exemption). The exemption is a lifetime limit for each individual, so how the land is owned (one name, jointly with a spouse, or through a family farm corporation or partnership) matters. How it interacts with past claims, other income and minimum tax is a job for your accountant.

## Inherited Farmland: What Happens When a Parent Dies

When a farmland owner dies, the tax treatment depends a great deal on who inherits the land and how it has been used.

As a general rule, capital property is treated as if it were sold at fair market value just before death, and the gain is reported on the deceased's final return. Farmland is one of the exceptions. CRA allows a tax-deferred transfer of a deceased parent's Canadian farm property to a child if the land was used mainly in a farming business on a regular and ongoing basis by the parent, their spouse or one of their children, and the transfer happens within 36 months of the death. The executor can choose the transfer amount within a set range, and the child takes over the land at that amount.

This matters later: when the heir sells, their gain is measured from the transfer amount, and in some situations the land may still qualify for the heir's own lifetime exemption. If you've inherited farmland, ask your accountant what amount was used on the estate return before you set a price.

## Transferring Farmland to a Child While You're Alive

You don't have to wait for an estate to pass land to the next generation on a tax-deferred basis.

CRA's rules let a parent transfer qualifying Canadian farmland to a child (a definition that includes grandchildren and great-grandchildren in some cases) who was a Canadian resident just before the transfer. The transfer can be made at any amount between the land's adjusted cost base and its fair market value, which lets families choose how much gain to trigger now. A parent who still has room in their lifetime exemption might choose to use part of it on the transfer, while another family might defer the whole gain until the child sells.

If a buyer, including a family member, pays you over several years, a capital gains reserve may let you spread the gain over those years, within limits CRA sets. Each choice has trade-offs for parent and child, so plan well before any land changes hands.

## Succession Planning Starts Years Before the Sale

The best tax outcomes usually come from decisions made five or ten years before a farm is sold, not five weeks.

Farm succession planning covers more than tax: who will farm, how non-farming children are treated fairly, what the parents need to retire on and whether the land will be sold, rented or passed down. Many families put these conversations off until a health scare or an unexpected offer forces the issue.

## What to Do Before You List Your Farmland

A little preparation before you list can protect options you can't get back after the sale.

1. **Find your cost records.** Dig out what you paid for each quarter, when you bought or inherited it, and any major improvements. For inherited land, the value used on the estate return is likely your cost.
2. **Ask your accountant about QFFP and the LCGE.** Bring your land details and history of how the land has been farmed or rented. Your accountant can tell you whether the land is likely to qualify and how much exemption room you have left.
3. **Talk to a lawyer about succession and your will.** If family members are involved, a lawyer can make sure transfers, wills and any agreements line up with the tax plan. It's much easier to sort this out before an offer arrives.
4. **Get a current read on market value.** Your accountant and lawyer will need a realistic fair market value to plan around. Grant can provide a market evaluation of your land, and a formal appraisal may be needed for some transfers.
5. **Choose your timing.** Closing in December versus January can move a gain into a different tax year. Saskatchewan's busiest farmland market runs from November to March, as we explain in our guide to the [best time to sell farmland in Saskatchewan](https://mysaskfarm.com/best-time-sell-farmland-saskatchewan/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I avoid capital gains on inherited farmland in Canada?

You may not be able to avoid them entirely, but they can often be reduced or deferred. If farmland passes to a child and meets CRA's farming-use conditions, the transfer can be tax-deferred, and the heir's gain on a later sale is measured from the transfer amount. Qualified farm or fishing property may also be eligible for the lifetime capital gains exemption. Talk to your accountant before selling inherited land.

### What is qualified farm property?

Qualified farm or fishing property (QFFP) is CRA's term for farm property that can be eligible for the lifetime capital gains exemption. Generally, the land must have been owned by you or a qualifying family member for at least 24 months and used mainly in a farming business in Canada, with a family member actively engaged on a regular and ongoing basis. Some land must also meet an income test.

### How much is the lifetime capital gains exemption for farmland?

CRA lists the lifetime capital gains exemption for qualified farm or fishing property at $1,250,000 for 2025, which is a maximum deduction of $625,000. The limit is indexed to inflation starting in 2026. It is a per-person lifetime limit, so past claims reduce what's left.

### Can I sell my farmland to my children for less than it's worth?

CRA allows a parent to transfer qualifying farmland to a child at any amount between its adjusted cost base and fair market value, which can defer some or all of the gain. Rules on residency and farm use apply, so have a lawyer and accountant set it up.

### Do I pay capital gains if I sell farmland I've been renting out?

Usually yes, a sale of rented farmland still creates a capital gain. Whether that land qualifies for the lifetime capital gains exemption depends on when you acquired it and how it has been used, so rented land needs a careful look from your accountant before you list.
