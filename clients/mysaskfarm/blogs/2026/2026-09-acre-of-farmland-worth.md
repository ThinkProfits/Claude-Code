# How Much Is an Acre of Farmland Worth in Saskatchewan?

> **Quick Answer:** Statistics Canada puts the average value of an acre of farmland and buildings in Saskatchewan at $2,943 as of July 1, 2025, about half the national average of $5,643. The real answer for your land depends on soil class, moisture zone, land type and how many active buyers farm nearby, so an acre of farmland in Saskatchewan can sell well above or well below that average.

Ask ten people at the coffee shop how much an acre of land is worth and you'll get ten answers, plus a story about a quarter that sold for far too much back in 2014. For Saskatchewan farmland, the honest answer starts with a provincial average and ends with your specific parcel. Whether you're a buyer pricing an offer on the land next door or a seller benchmarking before you list, the gap between those two numbers is where the money is. This guide covers the latest verified figures, the factors that push an acre above or below the average, and how to get a realistic number for your own land. It's written with Grant Ostapowich of MySaskFarm.com, who has farmed in Saskatchewan for more than 30 years and has sold farmland across the province for 17.

## The Average Price of an Acre of Farmland in Saskatchewan

The most recent official figure is $2,943 per acre, and it has climbed every year this decade.

Statistics Canada tracks the [value per acre of farm land and buildings](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/t1/tbl1/en/tv.action?pid=3210004701) for every province, measured at July 1 each year. Here's how Saskatchewan has moved:

| Year (July 1) | Saskatchewan, $ per acre | Canada, $ per acre |
|---|---|---|
| 2021 | $1,798 | $3,947 |
| 2022 | $2,050 | $4,527 |
| 2023 | $2,385 | $4,957 |
| 2024 | $2,647 | $5,302 |
| 2025 | $2,943 | $5,643 |

That's roughly an 11% gain from 2024 to 2025, and about 64% since 2021. If you want the story behind those numbers (what's driving demand and whether the run is slowing), our breakdown of [Saskatchewan farmland prices](https://mysaskfarm.com/saskatchewan-farmland-prices/) covers the trend in detail.

Keep two things in mind before you multiply your acres by $2,943. First, the StatCan figure blends land and buildings, so it includes yard sites and bins. Second, it averages every kind of farmland in the province, from heavy black soil near Melfort to dry pasture in the southwest. Very few actual parcels sell at the average.

## Why Your Acre Isn't the Average Acre

The price per acre for a specific parcel comes down to what the land can grow, where it sits and who wants it.

### Soil Class and Productivity

Soil quality is the single biggest driver of farmland value. A quarter with mostly high-rated soil and few stones will out-yield a quarter of lighter, salty or stony ground year after year, and buyers price that in. Two quarters a few kilometres apart can sell for very different per-acre prices because of soil alone, which is why a neighbour's sale is a starting point rather than a verdict on your land.

### Moisture Zone: North vs South of Highway 1

Saskatchewan's moisture picture changes a lot from the black and dark brown soil zones in the north and east to the brown soil zone in the southwest. More reliable moisture generally means more reliable yields, so land in the wetter parkland belt usually draws stronger demand. Grant sees the most buyer activity in the RMs north of Highway 1, and that competition shows up in the price.

### Cropland, Pasture and Hunting Land

Cultivated cropland, native pasture, hay land and recreational or hunting quarters each attract different buyers with different budgets. A grain farmer expanding acres is not shopping the same way as a rancher looking for grass and water, or a buyer who wants a quarter of bush and sloughs for hunting. Pasture has its own logic, which we cover in our look at [why pastureland remains the rancher's stronghold](https://mysaskfarm.com/the-grass-ceiling-why-pastureland-remains-the-ranchers-stronghold/).

### Who Farms Next Door

Location matters in a very farm-specific way: the most motivated buyer for your land is often the operator whose equipment already drives past it. When two or three expansion-minded neighbours want the same quarter, the price per acre can move well past what the soil alone would suggest. In areas with fewer active buyers, even very good land can take longer to sell.

### Cultivated Acres vs Titled Acres

A quarter section is roughly 160 titled acres, but buyers usually focus on how many of those acres are actually in crop. Sloughs, bush, a yard site and road allowances all reduce the cultivated count. Two quarters with the same title acreage can have very different values once you look at what can actually be seeded.

## Your SAMA Assessment Is Not Your Market Value

A SAMA assessment tells you how your land's productivity compares with other land, not what a buyer will pay for it.

The Saskatchewan Assessment Management Agency assesses agricultural land under a regulated standard rather than market value. According to [SAMA's methods of assessment](https://www.sama.sk.ca/property-owner-services/understanding-assessment/methods-assessment), cultivated land is rated with a soil classification system tied to long-term wheat yields, and pasture is rated on its carrying capacity. That makes your assessment notice useful for comparing the relative quality of two parcels, but it can sit far below what the land actually sells for.

So if you've been pricing your land off your tax notice, you're probably underestimating it. Buyers and lenders look at recent comparable sales, not assessments, when they decide what an acre is worth.

## How Buyers and Sellers Should Use Per-Acre Numbers

The same per-acre figure means different things depending on which side of the deal you're on.

**If you're buying,** a per-acre price is only useful once you know what's behind it. Ask how many of the acres are cultivated, what the soil ratings look like and what nearby land has actually sold for in the last year or two. It also helps to run the numbers on what the land can earn, either from your own crop or from cash rent, so you know where your walk-away price sits. You can browse current [Saskatchewan farmland listings](https://mysaskfarm.com/listings/) to see how asking prices compare across regions.

**If you're selling,** resist the urge to anchor on the single highest sale you've heard about. That parcel may have had better soil, a bidding war between neighbours or a buyer with a very specific reason to pay up. A realistic number grounded in true comparables tends to attract serious buyers faster, and timing matters too: our guide to the [best time to sell farmland in Saskatchewan](https://mysaskfarm.com/best-time-sell-farmland-saskatchewan/) explains why November to March is usually the busiest window.

## How to Get a Realistic Number for Your Own Land

The fastest way to an honest per-acre figure is to combine your land's details with recent sales near it.

1. **Gather your land information.** Pull together the legal land descriptions, your SAMA assessment notices, cultivated acres per quarter and a few years of crop or rental history. Having this ready means a realtor or appraiser can give you a useful answer in one conversation instead of three.
2. **Look at recent sales in your RM.** Sales of similar land in your rural municipality over the last 12 to 24 months are the best evidence of value. Our RM pages, like the one for the [RM of Corman Park](https://mysaskfarm.com/rm-of-corman-park/), show current listings by area, and Grant can walk you through what has actually closed nearby.
3. **Get a market evaluation or a formal appraisal.** A market evaluation from a farmland realtor gives you a working range for planning or listing. A formal appraisal is a paid, written opinion of value, and you'll usually want one for estate, lending or family-transfer situations.
4. **Decide what the number is for.** A value for selling, for buying out a sibling or for estate planning may each call for a different level of detail. Be clear about the purpose up front so you get the right kind of answer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much is an acre of land worth in Saskatchewan?

Statistics Canada's most recent figure puts the average value of farmland and buildings in Saskatchewan at $2,943 per acre as of July 1, 2025. Good cropland in high-demand areas can sell well above that, while lighter land and some pasture sell below it. A recent comparable sale in your own RM is a better guide than the provincial average.

### How much is a quarter section of land worth in Saskatchewan?

A quarter section is about 160 acres, so at the 2025 provincial average of $2,943 per acre a full quarter would work out to roughly $470,000. In practice, buyers price on cultivated acres and soil quality, so two quarters can sell for very different amounts. [GRANT TO CONFIRM: optional typical quarter price range in his core area.]

### How many acres are in a quarter section?

A standard quarter section is 160 acres, or one-quarter of a 640-acre section under the prairie survey system. The actual titled acreage can be a little higher or lower because of road allowances and survey adjustments. Cultivated acres are often well below 160 once sloughs, bush and yard sites are taken out.

### Is my SAMA assessment the same as what my land would sell for?

No. SAMA assesses agricultural land under a regulated standard based on productivity (soil ratings tied to long-term wheat yields for cropland, carrying capacity for pasture), not on market value. Your assessment is useful for comparing land quality, but sale prices are set by buyers and usually come in well above the assessed value.

### How can I find out what farmland is worth per acre in my area?

Start with sales of similar land in your rural municipality over the past one to two years, then adjust for your soil, cultivated acres and improvements. A farmland realtor who works your area can pull those comparables and give you a working range, and a formal appraisal is the next step if you need a written value for estate, lending or a family transfer.
