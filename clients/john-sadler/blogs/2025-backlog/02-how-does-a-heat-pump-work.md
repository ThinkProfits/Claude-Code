# How Does a Heat Pump Work? A Surrey, BC Guide

> **Quick Answer (Preformatted block):** How does a heat pump work? It uses electricity to move heat instead of making it. Refrigerant absorbs warmth from the outdoor air, a compressor concentrates that warmth, and an indoor coil releases it into your home. In summer, a reversing valve runs the same cycle backward to pull heat out of the house.

More homeowners in Surrey, White Rock and Langley are asking us about heat pumps. Most people know a heat pump can both heat and cool, but how does a heat pump work when the air outside feels cold and damp? The short answer is that it acts as a heat mover, not a heat maker. This guide walks through the refrigerant cycle in plain language, what the outdoor unit is doing on a frosty January morning, why it sometimes gives off a cloud of vapour, and how efficiency is measured. If you're weighing a heat pump against a furnace or an air conditioner, those decisions are covered in separate articles linked below.

## Heat Pump Explained: Moving Heat Instead of Making It

A heat pump moves heat that already exists from one place to another, which is why it can deliver more heat than the electricity it uses. A gas furnace makes heat by burning fuel, and a baseboard heater makes it with a resistance element. A heat pump spends its electricity running a compressor and fans, and that work carries heat from outside to inside.

Natural Resources Canada describes a heat pump as an electrically driven device that extracts heat from a low-temperature place and delivers it to a warmer one ([NRCan, Heating and cooling with a heat pump](https://natural-resources.canada.ca/energy-efficiency/energy-star-canada/about/energy-star-announcements/publications/heating-and-cooling-heat-pump/6817)). Your fridge works the same way, which is why the coils on the back feel warm.

## The Refrigerant Cycle, Step by Step

So how does a heat pump work from the inside? Every system runs a closed loop of refrigerant that changes between liquid and gas as it travels between the outdoor and indoor units, soaking up heat or giving it off with each change. Here is the loop in heating mode.

### 1. The Outdoor Coil Absorbs Heat (Evaporator)

Cold, low-pressure liquid refrigerant flows through the outdoor coil while a fan pulls outside air across it. Because the refrigerant is colder than the outdoor air, even chilly air makes it boil into a vapour, and that change captures heat from outside.

### 2. The Compressor Raises the Temperature

The vapour then travels to the compressor, which squeezes it into a smaller volume. Compressing a gas makes it hotter, the same way a bicycle pump warms up as you use it. This is where most of the system's electricity goes.

### 3. The Indoor Coil Releases Heat (Condenser)

The hot gas moves to the indoor coil, which sits in your ductwork or inside a wall-mounted unit. A fan pushes room air across the coil, the air picks up the heat, and the refrigerant condenses back into a liquid.

### 4. The Expansion Valve Resets the Loop

The liquid refrigerant then passes through an expansion valve that drops its pressure suddenly, chilling it well below the outdoor temperature so it's ready to absorb heat again. The loop repeats for as long as your thermostat calls for heat.

## Heating Mode vs Cooling Mode

A single component, the reversing valve, lets the same equipment heat your home in winter and cool it in summer by controlling which direction the refrigerant flows. In cooling mode, the valve flips the loop: the indoor coil absorbs heat from your rooms and the outdoor unit releases it outside, exactly how a central air conditioner works. If you're deciding whether you need heating and cooling or just cooling, our article on [choosing between a heat pump and an AC](https://www.johnsadler.ca/blog/heat-pump-vs-ac-unit/) covers that decision.

## How Does a Heat Pump Work in Winter?

Even cold air holds usable heat, and the refrigerant is kept colder than that air so it can keep absorbing it. As temperatures drop, there's less heat to collect, so capacity and efficiency fall. Natural Resources Canada notes that standard air-source heat pumps may need supplemental heat when outdoor temperatures fall to between -10°C and -15°C ([NRCan, Heat pumps](https://natural-resources.canada.ca/energy-efficiency/home-energy-efficiency/canada-greener-homes-initiative/heat-pumps)).

That's good news for the Lower Mainland. Our winters are mild and wet by Canadian standards, and cold snaps well below -10°C are uncommon and usually short, so an air-source heat pump here spends most of the season in conditions it handles well.

### Cold-Climate Heat Pumps

A cold climate heat pump uses a variable-speed compressor and improved coils to keep producing heat in much colder weather, and NRCan says these models can operate as cold as -30°C. In Surrey, that mostly means extra capacity in reserve during a cold snap rather than a requirement, and we weigh it against your insulation and backup heat plan when sizing a system.

### Heat Pump Defrost Mode (and Why Your Unit Is Steaming)

On damp mornings near freezing, which Surrey has plenty of, frost builds up on the outdoor coil and blocks airflow. The system clears it with a defrost cycle: it briefly switches to cooling mode to send hot refrigerant to the outdoor coil, and the outdoor fan shuts off while the frost melts. NRCan's guide says a defrost cycle can start every 30, 60 or 90 minutes, depending on the climate and system design.

During defrost you may see steam rising from the outdoor unit and hear a whoosh as the reversing valve shifts. That's normal: it's melted frost evaporating off a warm coil. A coil that stays encased in thick ice for hours is not normal and is worth a service call.

## Heat Pump Efficiency: What COP Means

Heat pump efficiency is usually described by its coefficient of performance, or COP: the heat delivered divided by the electricity used. A COP of 3 means three units of heat for every unit of electricity, while an electric baseboard heater sits at a COP of 1.

COP changes with the weather. [NRCan's heat pump guide](https://natural-resources.canada.ca/energy-efficiency/energy-star-canada/about/energy-star-announcements/publications/heating-and-cooling-heat-pump/6817) puts the typical COP of air-source heat pumps between 2.0 and 5.4 at 8°C, dropping to between 1.1 and 3.7 at -8°C. Those are ranges across many models, not a promise for any one unit. Spec sheets also list seasonal ratings, HSPF for heating and SEER for cooling, while gas furnaces use a different scale explained in our guide to [AFUE ratings](https://www.johnsadler.ca/blog/afue-ratings-explained/).

## Backup Heat and Dual-Fuel Systems

Most heat pumps are paired with a second heat source for the coldest days, usually one of two setups:

- **Electric backup heat:** Resistance elements in the indoor air handler switch on alongside the heat pump when it can't keep up. It's simple, but those elements run at a COP of 1, so they cost more to run per unit of heat.
- **Dual-fuel (hybrid) with a gas furnace:** The heat pump handles most of the season, and the gas furnace takes over below a set outdoor temperature. NRCan notes that in a hybrid system, either the heat pump or the furnace runs, not both at once.

Dual-fuel setups suit the many Lower Mainland homes that already have natural gas and ductwork. The switchover temperature, often called the balance point, is set on the thermostat based on your home's heat loss and energy rates. ## Ducted, Ductless, Air-Source and Ground-Source

All heat pumps run the same refrigerant cycle but differ in where heat is collected and how it's delivered.

### Ducted vs Ductless

In a ducted system, the indoor coil sits in the ductwork next to your furnace or air handler and uses your existing vents. A ductless system, or mini-split, uses wall-mounted indoor units in individual rooms, connected to the outdoor unit by refrigerant lines. Ductless suits homes without ducts, such as those heated by a boiler or radiant floors, and our article [is a ductless split right for me?](https://www.johnsadler.ca/blog/ac-heat-pump-ductless-split/) covers which homes suit which system.

### Air-Source vs Ground-Source

Air-source heat pumps, the focus of this guide, collect heat from outdoor air. Ground-source (geothermal) systems collect it from the earth or groundwater through buried piping loops. Because ground temperatures are steadier, NRCan notes they need no defrost cycle and typically hold a COP around 3 in the coldest months. The trade-off is a much larger installation, so air-source is the practical choice for most Lower Mainland lots.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a heat pump work when it's below freezing?

Yes. Outdoor air still holds usable heat below 0°C. Standard air-source models may need supplemental heat somewhere between -10°C and -15°C, while cold-climate models can operate down to about -30°C.

### Why is steam coming out of my heat pump in winter?

Steam from the outdoor unit usually means the heat pump is in defrost mode. Hot refrigerant melts frost on the outdoor coil, and the meltwater evaporates as a visible cloud. This can happen every 30 to 90 minutes in cold, damp weather and lasts only a few minutes.

### Is it normal for a heat pump to run for long stretches in winter?

Yes. A heat pump delivers a steady stream of warm air rather than short, hot blasts like a furnace, so long run times are expected in cold weather. If the house isn't reaching the thermostat setting or the outdoor coil stays iced over for hours, have it checked.

### Do I need backup heat with a heat pump?

Most systems are installed with backup, either electric elements in the air handler or a gas furnace in a dual-fuel setup. In mild coastal winters the backup runs only a small part of the season, mainly during cold snaps.

### How long does a heat pump last?

Natural Resources Canada puts the typical service life of an air-source heat pump at 15 to 20 years. Yearly maintenance, including clean coils, fresh filters and a clear outdoor unit, helps a system reach the upper end of that range.
