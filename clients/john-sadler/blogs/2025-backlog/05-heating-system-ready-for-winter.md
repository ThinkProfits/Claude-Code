# How to Get Your Home Heating System Ready for Winter

> **Quick Answer (Preformatted block):** To get your heating system ready for winter, start by identifying what kind of system you have, because a gas furnace, a boiler, a heat pump and a tankless water heater each need different checks. Do the simple homeowner steps for your system (filters, pressure, clearances, test run), then book an annual tune-up with a licensed technician before the first cold snap.

Most winter prep checklists assume every home has a furnace. Plenty of homes around Surrey, White Rock and Langley don't. Some run on a boiler or radiant in-floor heat, some have a heat pump with a gas furnace as backup, and many rely on a Navien tankless unit for hot water. If you want to get your heating system ready for winter, the right checklist depends on which of these you have.

This guide is organised by system type, so you can skip straight to yours, with the checks you can do yourself and the work best left to a licensed technician. If you are after general fall jobs like disconnecting hoses and insulating pipes, we cover those in our guide to [winterizing your plumbing](https://www.johnsadler.ca/blog/winterize-your-plumbing/).

## Step 1: Know What Kind of Heating System You Have

If you are not sure what heats your home, a quick look in your mechanical room usually answers it.

- **Gas furnace:** a cabinet connected to ductwork with a filter slot. Warm air comes out of floor or wall registers.
- **Boiler or combi boiler:** a unit with water pipes, a pressure gauge and often zone valves or a manifold. Heat comes from radiators, baseboards or warm floors.
- **Heat pump:** an outdoor unit with a large fan, connected to an indoor air handler, a furnace (a "dual-fuel" setup) or wall-mounted heads.
- **Tankless water heater:** a compact wall-mounted unit, often a Navien, that heats water on demand.

If your home has two of these working together, such as a heat pump paired with a furnace, follow the checklist for each one.

## Gas Furnace Winter Checklist

A gas furnace needs clean airflow, a clear venting path and a yearly safety inspection.

### 1. Replace the Filter Before the First Cold Week

A clogged filter is the most common reason a furnace overheats and shuts itself off. Swap it now, then check it monthly through the heating season and replace it whenever it looks grey and dusty.

### 2. Do a Test Run

Set the thermostat a few degrees above room temperature and let the furnace run for 15 to 20 minutes. A furnace that starts, stops after a minute or two and repeats is short cycling, and it's worth getting checked before the cold weather arrives.

### 3. Check the Outdoor Vent and Condensate Line

High-efficiency furnaces vent through PVC pipes on an outside wall. Make sure leaves, plants and stored items are not blocking them. Also check the small condensate drain line inside, since a blocked drain can shut the furnace down.

### 4. Book the Furnace Tune-Up

A furnace tune-up is where the safety checks happen. In BC, servicing gas appliances is a job for a licensed gas fitter, and Technical Safety BC recommends annual inspections of gas appliances by a licensed gas contractor. Not sure if yearly is really needed? Read [how often a furnace should be serviced](https://www.johnsadler.ca/blog/furnace-service-frequency/).

## Boiler, Combi Boiler and Radiant In-Floor Checklist

Hot water heating systems rely on the right water pressure and air-free piping, and a few simple checks will tell you whether yours is ready.

[IMAGE: Close-up of a boiler pressure gauge and zone valves in a South Surrey home | alt: Boiler pressure gauge and zone valves checked before winter | file: boiler-maintenance-pressure-gauge.jpg]

### 1. Read the Pressure Gauge

Find the pressure gauge on the front panel or nearby piping, and compare the reading to the normal range in your owner's manual or marked on the gauge. Pressure that keeps dropping over days or weeks usually points to a leak or a failing expansion tank, so call us rather than repeatedly topping it up.

### 2. Bleed Air From Radiators and Baseboards

If a radiator or baseboard is warm at the bottom but cool at the top, there is usually air trapped inside. With the heat off and the system cool, open the bleed valve slightly until water appears, then close it. Bleeding lowers system pressure, so check the gauge again afterwards.

### 3. Check Zone Valves and Room Thermostats

Turn up each zone's thermostat, one at a time, and confirm that area warms up. A zone that stays cold often has a stuck zone valve or a circulator pump issue. For radiant in-floor systems, check that each floor zone warms up evenly; a cold patch can point to air in the loop or a manifold valve that needs attention.

### 4. Combi Boilers: Watch Your Hot Water Too

A combi boiler heats both your home and your tap water. If showers are running lukewarm or the unit is showing error codes, have it checked before winter demand peaks. ## Heat Pump Winter Maintenance

A heat pump has to pull heat from cold outdoor air all winter, so most of your prep happens outside, around the unit itself.

[IMAGE: Outdoor heat pump unit with clear space around it beside a Langley home | alt: Outdoor heat pump unit kept clear of leaves and debris for winter | file: heat-pump-winter-maintenance.jpg]

### 1. Clear the Outdoor Unit

Rake away leaves, trim back shrubs and keep bins, firewood and patio furniture away from the unit. Your installation manual lists the minimum clearance for your model; keep that space open all winter. After a snowfall, gently clear snow away from the sides and top so air can move through the coil.

### 2. Know That Defrost Mode Is Normal

When it is cold and damp, frost can build up on the outdoor coil. According to [Natural Resources Canada](https://natural-resources.canada.ca/energy-efficiency/energy-star/heating-cooling-heat-pump), the system clears this by briefly switching into cooling mode, and the outdoor fan shuts off during the defrost cycle. A few minutes of this is expected; a unit that stays encased in ice is not, and needs a service call.

### 3. Understand Auxiliary and Backup Heat

Air-source heat pumps lose capacity in very cold weather, so most homes have a backup heat source. In an all-electric setup, that is usually electric elements, which your thermostat may show as "AUX." In a dual-fuel home, the furnace takes over below a set outdoor temperature, so both the heat pump and the furnace should be serviced before winter.

### 4. Change the Filter and Book Annual Service

Natural Resources Canada recommends changing or cleaning the heat pump's air filter every three months and having a qualified contractor service the unit every year. A pre-winter visit checks the refrigerant circuit, defrost controls and wiring before the heaviest load of the year. Learn more on our [heat pump services page](https://www.johnsadler.ca/services/heating-cooling/heat-pumps/).

## Tankless Water Heater and Navien Checklist

Winter is when tankless units work hardest, because colder incoming water has to be heated further for every shower.

### 1. Book Descaling and Annual Service

Minerals in water slowly coat the heat exchanger, which reduces efficiency and can trigger error codes. Navien's own maintenance guidance calls for annual service, including flushing the heat exchanger and cleaning the inlet water screens, air filter and condensate trap. Our Navien Certified Service Specialists handle this through our [Navien service team](https://www.johnsadler.ca/services/navien/).

### 2. Check the Venting and Condensate Drain

Condensing tankless units vent through PVC pipes and produce condensate. Keep the outdoor vent terminals clear and the condensate line draining freely, because a frozen or blocked line can shut the unit down.

## Every Home: Safety Checks and What a Pro Tune-Up Includes

A few checks apply to every home. Our [fall plumbing and heating prep post](https://www.johnsadler.ca/blog/prep-plumbing-heating-for-fall/) covers seasonal basics like registers and filters, so we will keep this short.

- **Test your carbon monoxide alarms:** Technical Safety BC recommends [Canadian-certified CO alarms on every floor and near bedrooms](https://www.technicalsafetybc.ca/public-safety/carbon-monoxide-safety), tested monthly and replaced every 7 to 10 years. Any home with a gas furnace, boiler, water heater or fireplace should have them.
- **Check the thermostat:** Replace the batteries, switch it to heat mode and confirm the system starts when the thermostat calls for heat.
- **Know what to do if you smell gas:** Leave the home right away without using light switches or phones inside, then call the FortisBC 24-hour emergency line at 1-800-663-9911 or 911 from outside. If a CO alarm sounds, get everyone outside first and call 911.

A professional tune-up covers what a homeowner cannot safely check. On gas appliances, our licensed gas fitters inspect the burners and ignition, test safety controls, check venting and look for heat exchanger wear. On boilers and heat pumps, they also check pressures, pumps, valves and electrical connections. If you rent out a suite or home, BC's Gas Safety Regulation requires annual inspection and servicing of gas appliances by a licensed contractor.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I get my heating system ready for winter?

Start by identifying your system type, since furnaces, boilers, heat pumps and tankless units need different checks. Replace filters, test-run the system for 15 to 20 minutes, clear outdoor vents and units, and test carbon monoxide alarms. Then book an annual tune-up with a licensed technician in early fall.

### How often should a furnace be tuned up?

A gas furnace should be inspected and serviced once a year. In BC, Technical Safety BC recommends annual inspections of gas appliances by a licensed gas contractor, and rental owners are required to have them serviced annually. Early fall is the most practical time, so problems are found before the heating season starts.

### What does a furnace tune-up include?

A licensed gas fitter inspects and cleans the burners, checks the ignition system and flame sensor, tests the safety limit controls and checks the venting. They also look for heat exchanger wear and check the blower and electrical connections.

### Is it normal for a heat pump to stop and frost up in winter?

Yes, a light layer of frost on the outdoor coil is normal in cold, damp weather. The heat pump clears it by running a short defrost cycle, during which the outdoor fan stops for a few minutes. If the unit stays covered in thick ice, or the defrost cycle never seems to finish, it needs a service call.
