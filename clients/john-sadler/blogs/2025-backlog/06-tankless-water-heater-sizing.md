# How to Find the Right Tankless Water Heater Size for Your Household

> **Quick Answer (Preformatted block):** What size tankless water heater do I need? Add up the hot water flow of every fixture your household runs at the same time, then choose a unit that can still deliver that flow when it heats your coldest winter water to about 49°C (120°F). For many Surrey family homes running two showers and a dishwasher at once, that points to a large gas condensing unit, once the gas line and venting are confirmed.

A tankless water heater never "runs out" the way a tank does, but it can only heat so much water per minute. Ask for more than that and the last shower of the morning turns lukewarm. So the real question behind "what size tankless water heater do I need" is how much hot water your home uses at its busiest moment, and how much heating that water needs on the coldest day of the year.

This guide walks through both numbers, shows a worked example for a typical South Surrey family home, and explains the gas and venting checks we do before recommending a unit for homes in Surrey, BC.

## The Two Numbers That Decide Tankless Water Heater Size

Every tankless sizing decision comes down to flow rate and temperature rise. Get both right and the unit keeps up through the busiest part of your day.

### Flow Rate: How Much Hot Water You Use at Once

Flow rate is measured in gallons per minute (GPM) or litres per minute (L/min). One GPM is about 3.8 L/min. The size you need depends on how many hot water fixtures could run at the same moment, like two showers and a dishwasher overlapping on a school morning.

### Temperature Rise: How Much the Water Has to Be Heated

Temperature rise is the gap between the cold water coming into your home and the hot water temperature you want at the tap. A common residential setting is 49°C (120°F), the figure Navien uses in its own example. The colder the incoming water, the bigger the rise, and the less hot water any unit can produce per minute.

[Navien's Canadian sizing guide](https://www.navieninc.ca/blog/notice/sizing-tankless-water-heaters) says the rise should be calculated for the coldest days of the year. Rather than guess at local water temperatures, our technicians measure your incoming water during a sizing visit and size for winter.

## How to Size a Tankless Water Heater in 4 Steps

You can work through the first three steps for a ballpark; the fourth needs a licensed gas fitter.

### 1. List the Fixtures That Run at the Same Time

Note which hot water fixtures overlap on a normal busy morning or evening. Navien's sizing guidance budgets 2 to 3 GPM for a shower, 2 to 3 GPM for a dishwasher and 3 to 5 GPM for a washing machine on a hot cycle. Only count what realistically overlaps, or you'll pay for capacity you never use.

### 2. Find Your Winter Temperature Rise

Subtract your coldest incoming water temperature from your target temperature. If water comes in at 7°C and you want 49°C, your rise is 42°C (about 75°F). Most online tankless water heater calculators guess at this number, which is where their results drift.

### 3. Match Your Demand to the Unit's Flow Chart

Manufacturers publish charts showing how many GPM each model delivers at different temperature rises. Find your rise, then pick a unit that meets your peak demand at that rise. The headline GPM on the box is usually based on a small rise you won't see in a BC winter.

### 4. Confirm the Gas Line and Venting

A larger unit only performs if it gets enough gas and can vent safely, which we cover below.

## A Worked Example: A South Surrey Family Home

Picture a two-storey South Surrey home with a family of four. On a school morning, two showers run at the same time while the dishwasher finishes a cycle.

- **Two showers:** a shower might flow around 2 GPM at the head, but part of that is cold water mixed in at the valve. In winter, roughly 1.6 GPM (about 6 L/min) of each shower comes from the water heater, so two showers need about 3.2 GPM (12 L/min).
- **Dishwasher:** at the low end of Navien's 2 to 3 GPM range, the dishwasher adds about 2 GPM (7.6 L/min) while it fills.
- **Peak demand:** together, that's about 5.2 GPM, or roughly 20 L/min of hot water at the busiest moment.

Now apply the 42°C (75°F) winter rise from Step 2. According to the [Navien NPE-2 series specification sheet](https://www.navieninc.com/downloads/npe-2-quickfacts-en/download), at a 75°F rise the NPE-180A2 delivers about 3.9 GPM (15 L/min), the NPE-210A2 about 4.7 GPM (18 L/min) and the NPE-240A2 about 5.2 GPM (20 L/min).

In this example, only the largest model covers the full peak on the coldest day. With a smaller unit, the second shower would run cooler on a January morning. If the household often adds a hot laundry load or fills a large soaker tub, two units working together may be the better fit.

Low-flow showerheads or staggered shower times can bring the size down, which is why we measure instead of assuming.

## What BTU Ratings Mean in Plain Terms

BTU input is how much gas a unit can burn per hour. Think of it as engine size, with GPM as the speed you get at a given temperature rise.

The NPE-A2 models on that spec sheet range from a maximum of 150,000 BTU/h (NPE-180A2) up to 199,900 BTU/h (NPE-240A2). A trade rule of thumb is that heating 1 GPM of water by 1°F takes about 500 BTU per hour. Our example of 5.2 GPM at a 75°F rise needs about 195,000 BTU/h of heat, which is why larger households usually land on a high-input gas condensing unit. Matching that with electricity would take roughly 57 kW, more than most homes' electrical service is set up to supply.

## Recirculation and ComfortFlow: Hot Water Sooner

Sizing decides how much hot water you get, while recirculation decides how quickly it reaches a far bathroom at the end of a long pipe run.

Navien's NPE-A2 models include ComfortFlow, a built-in buffer tank and recirculation pump that keeps hot water moving through the lines so it's closer to your fixtures when you need it. Recirculation uses some extra energy, so we set it up around how your household actually uses hot water. If slow hot water is your main complaint, see our post on [why your shower takes a long time to heat](https://www.johnsadler.ca/blog/shower-taking-a-long-time-to-heat/).

## Gas Lines, Venting and Why a Sizing Visit Beats a Calculator

A whole-home tankless unit can draw close to 200,000 BTU/h at full fire, far more than a typical gas hot water tank, and many existing gas lines were sized only for the old tank. If the line can't keep up, the unit never reaches its rated output. During a sizing visit, our licensed gas fitters check:

- **Gas line size and length:** pipe diameter, the length of the run from the meter and the other gas appliances on the line all affect how much gas reaches the heater. If the line is too small, a [gas line upgrade by our gas fitting team](https://www.johnsadler.ca/services/gas-fitting/) is part of the quote, not a surprise later.
- **Venting route:** condensing tankless units vent through plastic pipe to an outside wall or roof, so we confirm a code-compliant path with proper clearances from windows and doors.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What size tankless water heater do I need for a family of 4?

Size for the fixtures that run at the same time, not the number of people. A family of four running two showers and a dishwasher together needs roughly 5 GPM (about 20 L/min) of hot water. At a winter rise of about 42°C (75°F), that usually calls for a gas unit with close to 200,000 BTU/h of input.

### How many showers can a tankless water heater run at once?

It depends on the unit's output at your winter temperature rise. Each shower draws roughly 1.5 to 2.5 GPM of hot water once cold water is mixed in. A unit delivering about 5 GPM in winter can usually handle two showers at once, while three or more often call for two units installed together.

### What happens if a tankless water heater is too small?

An undersized unit can't heat water fast enough at peak demand, so the water turns lukewarm or swings in temperature when a second fixture opens. It shows up most in winter, when incoming water is coldest. The fix is a larger unit, a second unit or spreading out high-demand fixtures.

### Do I need a bigger gas line for a tankless water heater?

Often, yes. A whole-home tankless unit can use up to about 199,900 BTU/h at full fire, far more than most gas tanks. A licensed gas fitter checks the pipe size, the run from the meter and the other appliances on the line before confirming whether an upgrade is needed.

### Are online tankless water heater calculators accurate?

They give a rough starting point based on assumed water temperatures and typical fixture flow rates, but they can't measure your winter water temperature, check gas line capacity or see your venting options, and those three factors decide whether a unit performs as rated.
