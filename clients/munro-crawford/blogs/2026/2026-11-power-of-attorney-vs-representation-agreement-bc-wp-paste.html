Power of Attorney vs. Representation Agreement in BC: What’s the Difference?

Quick Answer: In BC, an enduring power of attorney lets someone manage your financial and legal affairs if you can no longer do it yourself, while a representation agreement lets someone make or help you make personal care and health care decisions. Neither document does the other’s job, so a complete incapacity plan usually includes both. Without them, your family may have to rely on default rules, a court application or the Public Guardian and Trustee.

Most people know they should “get a power of attorney.” Fewer realize that in British Columbia, a power of attorney only covers part of the picture. If a stroke, an accident or dementia leaves you unable to make decisions, someone needs authority over your bank accounts and property, and someone also needs authority to speak with your doctors and decide where you will live. BC handles those two jobs with two different documents under two different laws. This guide explains the power of attorney vs. representation agreement question in plain English. For a deeper look at powers of attorney alone, see our post on what a power of attorney is in BC.

The Short Version: Money vs. Health and Personal Care

One document deals with your money and the other deals with you. An enduring power of attorney is made under BC’s Power of Attorney Act and covers your financial affairs, including business, property and legal affairs. A representation agreement is made under the Representation Agreement Act and covers personal care and health care, with a limited financial option discussed below.

Enduring Power of Attorney: A Quick Summary

An enduring power of attorney names an “attorney” to handle your finances, and unlike an ordinary power of attorney, it keeps working if you become mentally incapable. Your attorney can pay bills, manage investments and handle real estate, but cannot make or change your will, and changing a beneficiary designation generally needs court approval.

It must be signed in front of two witnesses, or one if the witness is a lawyer or notary. Our power of attorney guide covers choosing an attorney and the signing steps. The key point here is that an enduring power of attorney gives no one authority over your health care.

What a Representation Agreement Does

A representation agreement names a “representative” who can help you make decisions, or make them for you, about your personal care and health care, such as consenting to medical treatment or arranging home support. BC’s Representation Agreement Act sets out two kinds of agreement, named after the sections that create them.

You can also register your agreement with the Nidus Personal Planning Resource Centre, a BC charity. Registration is optional, but it lets authorized health authorities, financial institutions and the Public Guardian and Trustee find your document, with your permission.

Section 7 vs. Section 9 Representation Agreements

The two types differ in what the representative can do and in how much capacity you need to sign.

Section 7: Standard Powers

A section 7 agreement can cover personal care, major and minor health care, getting legal services and instructing a lawyer (other than for a divorce), and the routine management of your financial affairs, such as paying bills and depositing pension and other income. It cannot authorize your representative to refuse health care that is necessary to preserve your life.

The important feature is the capacity test. Under section 8 of the Act, you can make a section 7 agreement even if you can no longer manage your finances or make a contract. The question is whether you can communicate that you want a representative, show choices and preferences, understand that the representative may make decisions affecting you, and trust that person. This makes a section 7 agreement a practical option when capacity is already declining, for example after a dementia diagnosis (see estate planning after an Alzheimer’s diagnosis).

If a section 7 agreement includes routine financial authority, you generally must also name a monitor to oversee the representative, unless the representative is your spouse, the Public Guardian and Trustee, a trust company or credit union, or two or more representatives must act together.

Section 9: Broader Personal and Health Care Powers

A section 9 agreement gives your representative wider authority over personal and health care. It can include deciding where you live, including a move to a care facility, who you associate with, and whether to give or refuse consent to health care, which can extend to refusing care needed to preserve your life. A section 9 agreement does not cover your finances.

The capacity test is stricter: you must be able to understand the nature and consequences of the proposed agreement. That is why it is usually best made while you are healthy.

Why a Complete Incapacity Plan Usually Needs Both

Since an enduring power of attorney covers finances and a representation agreement covers health and personal care, most adults benefit from having both. A section 7 agreement does include routine financial powers, but “routine” is the key word: larger transactions, such as selling your home, call for an enduring power of attorney.

You can name the same trusted person in both documents or split the roles. You can also add an advance directive, a written instruction giving or refusing consent to specific health care, so your representative and doctors know your wishes. What Happens If You Have Neither

If you become incapable without these documents, decisions are still made, just by someone you did not choose. For health care, a health care provider must choose a temporary substitute decision maker from a set list, starting with your spouse, then your children, parents and siblings, as long as they qualify. If no one qualifies, or family members disagree, the provider must turn to someone authorized by the Public Guardian and Trustee.

For finances, an ordinary power of attorney ends if you become mentally incapable. Your family may then need to apply to the BC Supreme Court to have a committee appointed, a process often called committeeship, or the Public Guardian and Trustee may be appointed to make financial and legal decisions for you. A court application takes time and money when your family is already under strain.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a section 7 and section 9 representation agreement?

A section 7 agreement covers personal care, health care, routine finances and legal matters, and it can be made by someone with reduced capacity. A section 9 agreement gives broader personal and health care powers, including refusing life-support if the agreement allows it, but it requires that you understand the nature and consequences of the agreement and it does not cover finances.

Do I need a lawyer to make a representation agreement in BC?

No. BC provides standard forms, and Nidus offers forms and guidance. A lawyer is helpful when your family situation is complex, when you want terms the standard forms do not include, or when there may be questions later about your capacity or the choice of representative.

Who makes medical decisions for me in BC if I don’t have a representation agreement?

A health care provider must choose a temporary substitute decision maker in a set order: your spouse, then your child, parent, sibling, grandparent, grandchild, other relative, close friend, or a person related to you by marriage. The person must be at least 19, have been in contact with you in the past 12 months, have no dispute with you, and be willing to carry out the duties the law sets out.
