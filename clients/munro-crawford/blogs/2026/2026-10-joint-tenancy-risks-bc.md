# Joint Tenancy Risks in BC: What to Know Before Adding a Child to Your Title

> **Quick Answer:** Adding an adult child to your home's title as a joint tenant can move the property outside your estate, but the joint tenancy risks in BC are real: a possible tax bill on your child's share, exposure to your child's creditors or separation, loss of control over selling or refinancing, and a legal presumption that your child holds the property in trust for your estate unless a gift can be proven. A will, a trust or properly documented intentions often reach the same goal with fewer surprises.

[IMAGE: An older couple reviewing property documents with a lawyer at a desk | alt: Parents reviewing joint tenancy risks with a BC wills and estates lawyer before adding a child to title | file: joint-tenancy-risks-bc.jpg]

It often starts with a sensible thought: "If I put my daughter on the title now, the house goes straight to her and we skip probate." For some families it works out as planned. For others, it creates tax, family and legal problems that surface years later, often after the parent has died and can no longer explain what they meant. If you have read our guide to [joint tenants vs. tenants in common in BC](https://munrocrawford.ca/blog/joint-tenants-vs-tenants-in-common-bc/), you already know how the right of survivorship works. This post is the next step. It sets out the main joint tenancy risks in BC so you can weigh them before you sign a transfer, not after.

## The Probate Benefit, in Brief

The appeal of adding a child as a joint tenant is simple: when one joint tenant dies, their interest passes to the surviving owner by right of survivorship, outside the will and outside the estate. That can reduce the value of the estate that goes through probate, as we explain in [avoiding probate in BC](https://munrocrawford.ca/blog/avoiding-probate-bc/) and [what assets are not subject to probate](https://munrocrawford.ca/blog/assets-not-subject-to-probate/). The savings are real, but they are only one line on the ledger.

## Risk 1: Capital Gains and Your Principal Residence Exemption

Adding your child with a real ownership interest is, for tax purposes, a gift of part of your property. The Canada Revenue Agency treats a gift of capital property as a sale at fair market value on the day you make it. If the home has been your principal residence for every year you have owned it, the gain on the share you give away is generally sheltered by the principal residence exemption, so there is often no immediate tax.

The bigger issue comes later. From the day your child is added, growth in the value of their share belongs to them. The exemption only shelters that growth if the home qualifies as your child's principal residence, which generally means your child (or their spouse or child) lived there during the year. Under the [CRA's principal residence rules](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/individuals/topics/about-your-tax-return/tax-return/completing-a-tax-return/personal-income/line-12700-capital-gains/principal-residence-other-real-estate.html), a family can also designate only one home per year, and an adult child who owns their own home will usually use the exemption there.

The result can be a capital gains bill on your child's share when the house is eventually sold. If the property is a cabin or rental rather than your home, adding your child can trigger tax on the transferred share right away. Our post on [gifting property to children in BC](https://munrocrawford.ca/blog/gifting-property-to-children-in-bc-how-why-it-helps-with-your-estate/) covers the gift side. Speak with your accountant first, because the result also depends on whether your child owns the share outright or holds it for you (see Risk 4).

## Risk 2: Your Child's Creditors and Separation

Once your child is on title, their interest is exposed to their own financial life. If your child is sued, falls behind on debts or goes bankrupt, a creditor may be able to pursue their interest in your home, which can complicate a sale or refinance even though you never borrowed a cent.

A separation can be just as disruptive. Under BC's [Family Law Act](https://www.bclaws.gov.bc.ca/civix/document/id/complete/statreg/11025_05), a gift to a spouse from a third party is generally excluded from the property divided on separation, but any increase in its value during the relationship can be divided, and the spouse claiming the exclusion has to prove it.

## Risk 3: You Lose Some Control

Adding a co-owner means sharing decisions. Selling the home, taking out a mortgage or line of credit, or arranging a reverse mortgage generally needs the signature of every registered owner. If your child disagrees or is hard to reach, a routine decision can stall.

It is also difficult to undo. You cannot take your child off title on your own; a transfer back usually needs their agreement or a court order. Severing the joint tenancy turns it into a tenancy in common but leaves your child on title.

There is a fairness question too. If survivorship applies, the home goes to the child on title regardless of what your will says about dividing your estate equally, so the other children may receive far less than you intended.

## Risk 4: The Resulting Trust Presumption From Pecore

This is the risk that surprises families most. In [Pecore v. Pecore, 2007 SCC 17](https://decisions.scc-csc.ca/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/2355/index.do), the Supreme Court of Canada confirmed that when a parent transfers property to an adult child for no payment, the law presumes the child holds it in trust for the parent. The older presumption that a transfer to a child is a gift now applies only to minor children. The case dealt with joint bank and investment accounts, but the Court described the resulting trust presumption as the general rule for gratuitous transfers.

In plain terms, after you die, your other children or your executor can argue that the house was never really your child's. Your child then has to prove, on a balance of probabilities, that you intended a gift. If they cannot, the property can be treated as part of your estate and may go through probate after all, defeating the reason for the transfer.

What decides these disputes is evidence of your intention at the time of the transfer. A signed declaration of your intention, a will that is consistent with it, and independent legal advice for both you and your child carry far more weight than a family member's memory of a conversation.

## Alternatives Worth Weighing

Joint tenancy is one tool, not the only one. Depending on your goals, these options can achieve similar results with fewer side effects:

- **A carefully drafted will:** A will lets you decide who receives your home and when, while the property stays in your name and under your control. Probate fees apply, but that cost can be smaller than the tax or litigation risk of joint title. Our [wills and estate planning](https://munrocrawford.ca/wills-and-estate-planning/) team can walk you through the trade-off.
- **A trust:** An [alter ego trust](https://munrocrawford.ca/blog/what-is-an-alter-ego-trust/), available if you are 65 or older, can hold your home outside your estate while you keep the right to live there. It comes with set-up costs and annual tax filings, so it suits some estates better than others.
- **Beneficiary designations:** For registered accounts and life insurance, [naming and updating beneficiaries](https://munrocrawford.ca/blog/updating-your-beneficiaries/) moves those assets outside the estate without touching your home's title.
- **Documented joint tenancy:** If joint tenancy still makes sense, put your intentions in writing with a lawyer so there is no doubt about whether you meant a gift.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I add my son or daughter to my house title in BC?

Yes. A parent can transfer part of their interest in a home to an adult child, usually as a joint tenant with right of survivorship, by registering a transfer with the Land Title and Survey Authority of BC. Get legal and tax advice first, because the transfer can have capital gains, creditor and estate consequences for you both.

### Will my child pay capital gains tax on the house?

Possibly. Your child's share is generally only covered by the principal residence exemption for the years the home qualifies as their own principal residence. If your child owns and lives in a different home, the growth in value of their share of yours may be taxable when the property is sold.

### Can I take my child off title later?

Not on your own. Removing a registered owner normally requires their signature on a transfer back to you, or a court order. Severing the joint tenancy changes it to a tenancy in common, but your child stays on title as a co-owner.
