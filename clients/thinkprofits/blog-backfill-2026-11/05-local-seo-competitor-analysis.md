# Local SEO Competitor Analysis: A Vancouver Step-by-Step

> **Quick answer:** A local SEO competitor analysis starts with the businesses that actually appear for your money terms from your customers' locations, not the ones you think of as rivals. Compare their Google Business Profile categories, reviews, service pages, links and citations against yours, then fix the gaps you control, starting with the cheapest ones.

[IMAGE: Hero placeholder, ThinkProfits branded graphic | alt: Local SEO competitor analysis worksheet comparing Vancouver map pack results | file: /images/blog/wp/tp-branded-placeholder.jpg]

Most business owners can name their competitors. They are often wrong about which ones matter in search. The company across the street that you lose quotes to may never appear in the map pack for "emergency plumber Burnaby," while a two-person shop in New Westminster with a well-built profile shows up every time.

A local SEO competitor analysis fixes that blind spot. Instead of guessing, you record who Google shows for the searches that bring you revenue, then compare what those businesses have that you don't. This guide walks through a method you can repeat every quarter, step by step, with a worksheet layout you can copy. It will not tell you how to "beat" anyone overnight, and it will not guarantee a ranking. It will show you where the real gaps are, so you stop spending money on the wrong fixes.

## Step 1: Find the competitors Google actually shows

Your search competitors are whoever appears for your money terms, from the places your customers search. That list is often different from your sales competitors, and it changes by neighbourhood.

Start with five to ten "money terms", the searches that lead to calls, bookings or quote requests ("furnace repair North Vancouver," not "HVAC tips"). Record the top three map pack results and the top five organic results for each.

Location matters more than most people expect. Google's own help page on [how local ranking works](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091) says local results are mainly based on relevance, distance and prominence. Distance means the map pack in Kerrisdale can look nothing like the map pack in Mount Pleasant for the same search.

A few ways to get a fairer view:

- **Search from where your customers are, not where you sit.** A rank tracker that checks results across a grid of points shows how visibility shifts across the city; we explain how to read one in our guide to [geo-grid reports for Vancouver](/digital-news/local-seo-geo-grid-report-vancouver/).
- **Separate map pack and organic results in your notes.** A business can lead the map pack while barely registering in organic results, and the fixes for each are different, so mixing them hides the pattern.
- **Count repeat appearances.** A business that shows up for seven of your ten money terms is a real search competitor; one that appears once is noise.

You should end up with three to five businesses that appear again and again. Those are the ones worth studying.

## Step 2: Compare Google Business Profile categories

Categories are the fastest comparison to make and often the most revealing. Google's help page on [managing your business category](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7249669) states plainly that the categories you select affect your local ranking.

Note each competitor's primary and additional categories, then compare against your own. Common findings:

- **A more specific primary category.** Google's own example is choosing "Nail salon" rather than the broader "Salon." If a competitor uses a more specific category for the work you both do, they may be a closer match for those searches. Google's guidance is to pick a specific category from its list that describes your core business.
- **A missing additional category.** If you offer a service that competitors list as a category and you don't, you may be leaving relevance on the table.
- **Category padding.** Some competitors list a dozen loosely related categories. Google's guidelines for representing your business ask owners to use as few categories as possible, so treat this as a risk on their side, not a tactic to copy.

One caution while you are in there: if a competitor has stuffed keywords or a city name into their business name, do not copy it. Google's guidelines say a business name should reflect the real-world name, and extra wording is not permitted.

## Step 3: Compare reviews on more than star rating

Reviews are a prominence signal, and Google's ranking help page says more reviews and positive ratings can help local ranking. Record more than the star rating, for each competitor and for yourself:

| What to record | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Total review count | Shows the overall gap you are working against. |
| Reviews in the last 90 days | Steady recent reviews suggest an active business; a big total with nothing new tells a different story. |
| Average rating | Useful, but a 4.7 with 300 reviews and a 4.9 with 20 reviews are not the same position. |
| Owner reply rate | Google says helpful replies can help a business stand out; it also shows prospects someone is paying attention. |
| Services and places mentioned in reviews | Reviews that name the job and the neighbourhood reinforce what the business does and where. |

The last row holds most of the insight. If a competitor's reviews keep mentioning "same-day" and "Burnaby," and yours mostly say "great service," you have learned something about how customers describe them. We cover this in more depth in [reviews as local entity signals](/digital-news/reviews-local-entity-signals-vancouver/).

Never respond to a review gap with paid or incentivized reviews. Google's Maps user-contributed content policy prohibits them and says incentivized reviews will be removed, and Google can restrict account privileges for policy violations.

## Step 4: Compare service pages and location pages

Organic results, and to a degree the map pack, depend on what your website says. This is often where the biggest gap sits.

For each competitor, look at the page their profile links to and the page that ranks organically for your money terms. Note:

- **Does each core service have its own page?** A competitor with a dedicated "drain cleaning" page is often a closer match for that search than a business with one "services" page listing ten things in a sentence.
- **How much does the page actually explain?** Look for process, pricing approach, service area and proof such as photos or project examples.
- **Are location pages useful or templated?** Ten city pages with the same text and a swapped city name are a weakness. If you plan to build location pages yourself, read our post on [service pages, location pages and blog posts](/digital-news/service-page-location-page-blog-post/) first, and check for [city landing page cannibalization](/digital-news/city-landing-page-cannibalization-search-console/) once they are live.

Then review your own pages the same way. Often the fix is not new pages but stronger versions of the ones you have, which is why our [local SEO work](/seo-services/local-seo/) usually starts with existing core pages before anything new.

## Step 5: Compare links and citations

Links and citations feed prominence. Google's ranking help page lists how many websites link to a business among the signals behind prominence, so a link gap is worth measuring.

For links, a tool such as Semrush can list the referring domains for each competitor's site. Skip the raw totals and look for local sources: neighbourhood associations, local news, suppliers, trade associations, sponsorship pages and community event listings.

For citations, check whether each competitor appears with consistent details in the main directories and industry listings for your trade. Then check yourself. Inconsistent names, old phone numbers and former addresses are a common and fixable problem; our post on [local business entity consistency](/digital-news/local-business-entity-consistency-ai-search/) explains why they matter beyond Google Maps.

## Step 6: Turn the findings into a short action list

The analysis only pays off if it produces a ranked list of fixes. Put everything in one worksheet and sort gaps by effort and likely impact.

A simple order that works for most small businesses:

1. **Profile fixes first.** Category, service, hours, photo and description updates take an afternoon and cost nothing.
2. **Review process second.** Set up a consistent way to ask every happy customer for a review, with a direct link, and reply to every review within a few days. This builds slowly, so start early.
3. **Core page improvements third.** Rewrite or expand the service pages where competitors are clearly more thorough. This takes longer and can support both organic and map pack visibility.
4. **Links and citations last.** Clean up inconsistent listings, then pursue the local link sources your competitors have that you could reasonably earn.

Re-run the same searches from the same points every quarter, so you compare like with like. If you want a starting point on the website side, our [free SEO audit tool](/free-seo-audit-tool/) gives you a quick read on technical and on-page issues.

## What a competitor analysis cannot tell you

A competitor analysis shows gaps, not guarantees. Closing every gap you find will not promise a top-three map pack position, because distance is a factor you cannot change, and Google says there is no way to pay for a better local ranking.

What it does well is stop you from guessing, so effort goes to the gaps that are actually there.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I find my local SEO competitors?

Search your five to ten most valuable service terms from the areas your customers are in, and record the map pack and organic results for each. The businesses that appear across most of those searches are your search competitors, even if you never compete with them for jobs.

### How often should I do a local SEO competitor analysis?

A full analysis once or twice a year is enough for most small businesses, with a lighter quarterly check of the same searches from the same points. Run a fresh one sooner if a new competitor suddenly appears in your map pack or your calls from Google drop noticeably.

### Why does a competitor with fewer reviews rank above me?

Reviews are only one part of prominence, and relevance and distance also count. The competitor may be closer to the searcher, use a more specific primary category, or have a stronger service page for that exact search. Check those three things before assuming you need more reviews.

### Should I copy a competitor's Google Business Profile categories?

Copy only the categories that genuinely describe your business. Google's guidance is to pick the most specific primary category that represents your core business and keep additional categories few. Adding categories for services you don't offer creates a mismatch customers may notice.
