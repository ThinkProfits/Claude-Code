# What to Look for When Hiring a Wildfire Mitigation Consultant

> **Quick Answer:** A good wildfire mitigation consultant visits your site, writes a report that ranks your specific risks, and gives you recommendations you can actually build, fund and maintain. Before you hire one in BC, check the scope and sample deliverables, the right credentials for the work (FireSmart training, a registered forest professional where fuel treatment is involved, and WorkSafeBC good standing for anyone working on site), and real field experience with how wildfire behaves around structures.

Hiring a wildfire mitigation consultant is usually a one-time decision that shapes years of spending. Their report decides where fuel gets removed, which buildings get sprinklers and how much water you need on hand. For a resort in the Okanagan, an industrial yard outside Kamloops or a lakeshore community with no fire hall nearby, that report is the plan. This guide covers what to check before you sign: scope, BC credentials, field experience, the questions worth asking and the red flags. If you want the basics of what consultants do first, our earlier post on [how a wildfire consultant can help protect your property](https://www.spiedr.com/blog/how-wildfire-consultants-help/) covers that ground.

## Start With the Scope: What Do You Actually Need Assessed?

The right consultant depends on what you are protecting, so define the job before you compare anyone. A single rural home, a 40-cabin resort and a remote camp with fuel storage are very different assignments, and a consultant who is excellent at one may be out of their depth on another.

Write down the structures, the people and the operations you need to keep running, then ask each candidate to describe how they would handle that specific site. A useful engagement for a commercial property usually has three parts:

- **Assessment:** a site visit that looks at fuels, terrain, structures, access roads and water sources. The consultant should walk the property in person, because slope, prevailing wind and the distance to the nearest fire response all change the answer.
- **Report:** a written document that ranks risks in priority order and explains why each one matters. Ask for a redacted sample report; if a candidate can't show you one, you have no way of judging what you'll receive.
- **Implementation:** practical help turning the report into work orders, equipment and budgets. Some consultants stop at the report, which is fine if you have the staff to carry it forward, but you should know that before you sign.

For follow-up visits, our post on [what businesses and municipalities should review each year](https://www.spiedr.com/blog/wildfire-risk-assessment-annual-review/) lists what a good annual review covers.

## Credentials That Carry Weight in BC

In BC, three credentials are worth checking, and each covers a different part of the job.

**FireSmart training.** FireSmart BC runs [Wildfire Mitigation Specialist training](https://firesmartbc.ca/wmp/wildfire-mitigation-specialists/), a 24-hour course with roughly 60 per cent field time, built around the FireSmart home assessment framework. It's a solid sign that someone understands how structures ignite. Keep in mind it was designed for homes, so for an industrial site, camp or multi-building property you also want evidence of commercial-scale work.

**A registered forest professional for fuel treatment.** If the plan involves thinning, prescribed fire or other fuel management on forested land, ask who will write and sign that part. Forest Professionals BC lists [wildland fire resiliency, risk reduction and prescribed fire as practice areas](https://www.fpbc.ca/practice-resources/practice-areas/), and some forestry work can only be done by a registered forest professional (an RPF or RFT). A consultant who doesn't hold that registration should be able to tell you which registered professional they work with.

**WorkSafeBC good standing.** If the consultant or their crew will do any work on your property, ask for proof they are registered and current with WorkSafeBC. WorkSafeBC notes that if a subcontractor isn't paying its premiums, [you could be liable for the premiums tied to their work](https://www.worksafebc.com/en/insurance/why-clearance-letter), and a clearance letter is how you check.

Also ask for proof of liability insurance that covers the type of work in the contract, especially if the scope includes tree falling, chipping or pile burning.

## Field Experience: Have They Seen Wildfire Up Close?

The best recommendations come from people who have watched fire move through real terrain. A consultant with wildland firefighting or structure protection experience knows how embers travel ahead of a fire front, which buildings tend to ignite first and how long it actually takes to get a pump running under pressure.

Ask candidates where their field experience comes from and how recently they worked an active fire season. Then ask them to explain a past recommendation in plain language: what they saw on the site, what they recommended and why. Someone who has been on the line will usually talk about specifics such as roof debris, wind exposure, water supply and access, rather than reciting a generic checklist. Local knowledge helps as well: grass fires around Kamloops behave differently from fires in Cariboo timber.

## Recommendations Should Match Your Water, Equipment and Access

A mitigation plan is only useful if it fits the resources your site actually has. Strong consultants look hard at water supply, equipment and response time, because those three things decide what is realistic.

For a remote property, the most important number is often how long it would take a fire department to arrive, if one can come at all. Where that response is more than 15 minutes away or doesn't exist, the plan needs to lean on your own water sources, pumps and sprinkler protection. Ask the consultant how they would size that equipment, how many structures it needs to cover and who on your team will be trained to deploy it.

It's also fair to ask whether a consultant sells equipment, and how that affects their advice. SPIEDR designs and builds [structure protection units](https://www.spiedr.com/structural-protection-unit/) and sprinkler systems, so we expect clients to ask us that question. The honest answer from any consultant should be the same: recommendations are sized to the site's risk, water and staffing, and you are free to take the report and act on it however you choose.

## Questions to Ask Before You Sign

These five questions separate a useful consultant from an expensive report.

1. **"Can I see a sample report for a site like mine?"** The sample shows you the level of detail, how risks are ranked and whether recommendations come with enough specifics to price. Ask for one from a similar property type, with client details removed.
2. **"Who will actually visit the site and write the report?"** Some firms sell the job with a senior person and send someone else to do the work. Get the names and credentials of the people doing your assessment in writing.
3. **"How do you handle fuel treatment that needs a registered forest professional?"** The answer should name the professional and explain how their prescription fits into the overall plan. If the answer is vague, expect delays when the work is ready to start.
4. **"What does implementation support look like after the report?"** Some consultants help you schedule work, train staff and set up equipment, while others hand over the document and leave. Neither is wrong, but you need to know which one you are paying for.
5. **"How will we know the work is done right?"** A good consultant will describe a follow-up visit or checklist to confirm the recommendations were carried out. That step matters if you plan to show the report to an insurer or a board.

## Red Flags to Watch For

Treat any of these as a reason to slow down.

- **Guarantees.** No consultant can promise that a property will survive a wildfire or that an insurer will lower your premium. Mitigation reduces risk; it doesn't remove it, and anyone who says otherwise is selling, not advising.
- **No site visit.** A desktop report built from satellite images can't see roof debris, a blocked gate or a pump that hasn't started since last season.
- **A generic checklist.** If the report could be pasted onto any property in the province, it wasn't written for yours.

## Making the Final Choice

The right consultant for your site is the one who visits in person, shows you real sample work, holds the right credentials for each part of the job and gives you recommendations you can actually carry out. Check the scope, confirm FireSmart training and forest-professional involvement where fuel work is planned, ask for WorkSafeBC good standing and liability coverage, and walk away from anyone who guarantees results. Off-season is the best time to do this, while there's time to fund and finish the work before next spring.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What does a wildfire mitigation consultant do?**
A consultant assesses a property's wildfire risk in person, writes a report that ranks those risks, and recommends practical fixes such as fuel reduction, building changes, water supply and sprinkler protection. Many also help with implementation, including scheduling work and training staff to use equipment.

**Do I need a Registered Professional Forester for wildfire mitigation work?**
Not for every part of the job, but Forest Professionals BC says some forestry work can only be done by a registered forest professional, and its practice areas include wildland fire risk reduction and prescribed fire. If your plan involves thinning or other fuel treatment on forested land, ask your consultant which registered professional will prepare that part.

**What is the difference between a FireSmart home assessment and a commercial wildfire assessment?**
A FireSmart home assessment follows a framework designed for individual homes, focused on how a house and the area right around it could ignite. A commercial assessment covers multiple structures, people on site, access roads, fuel and chemical storage, water supply and how operations shut down, so it needs a broader scope.

**Will hiring a wildfire consultant lower my insurance premium?**
It might help, but no consultant can promise it. Insurers set their own criteria. A detailed report and proof that the recommended work was completed give your broker better information to work with, so ask your insurer what documentation they accept before the assessment starts. Our guide to [how wildfire risk affects commercial insurance](https://www.spiedr.com/blog/wildfire-insurance-coverage-commercial-properties/) covers the wider picture.

**When is the best time to hire a wildfire consultant in BC?**
Fall and winter are usually best. Hiring in the off-season leaves time to budget for the recommendations, complete fuel work in suitable conditions and train staff before the next fire season starts in spring.
