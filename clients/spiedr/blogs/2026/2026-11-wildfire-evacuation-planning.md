# Wildfire Evacuation Planning for Large Properties and Remote Operations

> **Quick Answer:** Wildfire evacuation planning for a large or remote site means deciding in advance when you'll leave, how every person gets out, and what happens to the property once they're gone. In BC that means setting your own triggers ahead of official evacuation alerts and orders, mapping at least two ways out, keeping a live headcount of workers and guests, planning communications that work without cell service, and testing the plan before fire season.

A homeowner's evacuation plan fits on a fridge magnet. A wilderness lodge with 60 guests, a work camp at the end of a forestry service road or a ranch with livestock and fuel storage needs a lot more. Large properties and remote operations take longer to empty, often depend on one road and frequently sit beyond cell coverage and quick fire department response. This guide covers wildfire evacuation planning for those sites in BC: how the official alert and order system works, why you should set your own earlier triggers, and how to handle exits, headcounts, communications, shutdown and the buildings you leave behind. If you're looking for the household checklist instead, our post on [what to do under a wildfire evacuation alert](https://www.spiedr.com/blog/what-to-do-in-a-wildfire-evacuation/) covers it.

## How Evacuations Work in BC

In BC, evacuation alerts and orders come from local authorities, not from the fire crews on the ground. The BC Wildfire Service explains that it [doesn't implement evacuation orders or alerts itself](https://blog.gov.bc.ca/bcwildfire/how-do-evacuation-orders-and-alerts-work/); it recommends them to local governments and First Nations, and the decision weighs forecast fire behaviour and access to egress routes as well as current conditions.

The province uses three stages, set out in PreparedBC's [emergency evacuee guidance](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/safety/emergency-management/preparedbc/evacuation-recovery/evacuee-guidance):

- **Evacuation Alert:** be ready to leave on short notice. For a large site, your plan should already be well underway by this stage.
- **Evacuation Order:** you are at risk and must leave the area immediately. PreparedBC warns that staying in an area under an order puts you, your family and first responders in danger.
- **Evacuation Rescinded:** the risk to life and safety has passed. Some sites may still face road closures or restricted access, so plan the return as carefully as the exit.

There is also a tactical evacuation, used when a sudden threat leaves no time for written warnings and often coordinated by the RCMP or local police. A remote site should assume it could get very little warning and plan for that case.

## Set Your Own Triggers Ahead of the Official Ones

The official stages are built for whole communities, so a slow-to-empty site needs earlier triggers of its own. If moving everyone off your property takes three hours, waiting for an evacuation order leaves you no margin.

Work backwards from how long your full evacuation actually takes, then tie site actions to conditions you can watch. Useful triggers include a new fire within a set distance of your access road, an evacuation alert issued for a neighbouring area, smoke or fire activity reported on your only route out, or forecast winds pushing an existing fire toward you. Write each trigger down with the action it starts and the person who calls it.

A tiered approach works well for most operations:

1. **Watch:** a fire is reported in your region. The site lead checks EmergencyInfoBC and the BC Wildfire Service map at set times each day, confirms the headcount and makes sure vehicles are fuelled and pointed out.
2. **Prepare:** a trigger condition is met or an alert covers a nearby area. Non-essential staff and guests leave early, deliveries stop, and the crew begins shutdown and stages structure protection equipment.
3. **Go:** an alert or order covers your site, or your own trigger says the road is at risk. Everyone leaves on the planned route, and the last person out confirms the final headcount with the coordinator off site.

## Map Every Way Out, and Plan for Losing One

Your egress route is the single most important variable on a remote site. Many camps, ranches and resorts in the BC Interior rely on one road, and that road may run through the same timber the fire is burning.

Map your primary route and at least one alternate, even if the alternate is rough or seasonal. Note every gate, bridge, cattle guard and pinch point, and who holds the keys. Drive both routes before fire season and record how long they take with your actual vehicle mix, including buses or crew trucks pulling trailers.

For the case where no road is usable, identify a cleared, low-fuel area on site where people can gather while help is arranged, and talk to your local authority about whether air or water extraction is realistic for your location. Treat that area as a last resort. Keep it clear of fuel all season and protect it with sprinklers where possible, but never plan around staying behind.

## Account for Every Person on Site

An evacuation plan only works if you know exactly who is on the property at any moment. On large sites, the hardest people to account for are contractors, day visitors, guests and anyone working alone in the back country.

Use a sign-in and sign-out system that the coordinator can check quickly, whether that's a board at the gate, a radio log or an app that works offline. Set muster points that are easy to reach from every part of the property, and assign a named person to sweep each building or work area. Plan in advance for anyone who will need help to move, including guests with limited mobility and workers who may be injured.

For employers, this is also a legal duty. WorkSafeBC's [Occupational Health and Safety Regulation, Part 4](https://www.worksafebc.com/en/law-policy/occupational-health-safety/searchable-ohs-regulation/ohs-regulation/part-04-general-conditions) requires a risk assessment wherever workers may need to be evacuated (section 4.13). Where that assessment shows a need, the employer must put written procedures in place and assign a worker to coordinate them. Emergency drills must be held at least once a year, with records kept (section 4.14), and every worker must be instructed in the site's evacuation procedures (section 4.16).

## Plan Communications for When the Cell Signal Disappears

Remote sites can't count on cell service, and wildfire can take out towers and power lines that work fine on a normal day. Your plan needs a primary and a backup way to reach every crew, plus a way to reach the outside world.

Two-way radios on a dedicated channel are the standard for on-site crews, with satellite phones or satellite messaging for the site lead and anyone working far from base. Agree on fixed check-in times during a fire event, and decide what happens if a crew misses one. Keep a printed contact list with your local authority's emergency program, the nearest fire department, your regional district or First Nation, and an off-site contact who keeps the master headcount.

Don't rely on catching an alert by chance. Assign someone to watch EmergencyInfoBC, which posts alerts and orders from local authorities, at set times each day whenever there is fire activity in your region.

## Shutdown, Transport and What Stays Behind

Large operations rarely empty out by just locking the door. Fuel tanks, generators, processing equipment, livestock and chemical storage all need a shutdown sequence, and that sequence takes time you should measure in advance.

List each shutdown task in order, the person responsible and how long it takes. Match vehicle seats to your peak headcount, including guests and contractors, and decide in advance what happens to livestock or equipment that can't move in time. Our post on [wildfire protection for farms and ranches in BC](https://www.spiedr.com/blog/wildfire-protection-farms-ranches-bc/) covers the agricultural side in more depth.

The last step before leaving is protecting the structures you can't take with you. Sprinkler systems that run without anyone on site, such as [structure protection units](https://www.spiedr.com/structural-protection-unit/), roof sprinklers or a [1 KM WETLINE™](https://www.spiedr.com/sprinkler-trailers/1-km-wetline/) along a perimeter, should be staged and tested before fire season so starting them is a five-minute task during the Prepare stage, not an hour-long scramble. Water supply, pump fuel and who starts the system all belong in the written plan. Sprinklers improve a building's odds, but no system guarantees a structure will survive, which is why people always leave first.

## Test the Plan and Keep It Current

A plan that has never been tested is a guess. Run a tabletop session in the off-season and a live drill before the season starts, timing the shutdown, the headcount and the drive out with real vehicles.

Our post on [wildfire readiness drills for businesses](https://www.spiedr.com/blog/wildfire-readiness-drills-business/) walks through how to set one up. Review the plan whenever staffing, roads, buildings or equipment change, and fold it into your broader [wildfire emergency response plan](https://www.spiedr.com/blog/wildfire-emergency-response-plan-business/) so evacuation, structure protection and business recovery are covered in one document.

## Build Your Evacuation Plan Before Next Season

Good wildfire evacuation planning for a large or remote site comes down to a handful of decisions made well before smoke appears: when you'll leave, which roads you'll use, how you'll account for every person, how you'll talk to each other and what protects the property once you're gone. Set triggers earlier than the official alerts, map more than one way out, meet your WorkSafeBC duties and drill it before spring.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the difference between an evacuation alert and an evacuation order in BC?**
An evacuation alert means be ready to leave on short notice. An evacuation order means you are at risk and must leave the area immediately. For a large or remote site, the alert stage is when most of your plan should already be in motion, because emptying the property usually takes longer than it does for a single household.

**Who issues wildfire evacuation orders in BC?**
Local authorities, including regional districts, municipalities and First Nations, issue evacuation alerts and orders. The BC Wildfire Service recommends when they are needed, based on current conditions, forecast fire behaviour and access to egress routes. EmergencyInfoBC posts current alerts and orders.

**Does WorkSafeBC require a written evacuation plan?**
Under section 4.13 of the Occupational Health and Safety Regulation, employers must assess any workplace where workers may need to be evacuated. If the assessment shows that need, written procedures and a named coordinator are required. Section 4.14 also requires emergency drills at least once a year, with records kept.

**Can staff stay behind to run sprinklers during a wildfire evacuation order?**
No. PreparedBC warns that staying in an area under an evacuation order puts you and first responders in danger. Set up sprinkler protection so it can be started during the Prepare stage and keep running without anyone on site, then leave with everyone else.

**How often should a remote site update its wildfire evacuation plan?**
Review it at least once a year before fire season, alongside the annual drill WorkSafeBC requires. Update it right away whenever roads, buildings, staffing levels, equipment or emergency contacts change.
