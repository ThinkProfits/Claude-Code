# The Coquihalla in Winter: What Drivers and Truckers Need to Know

> **Quick Answer:** Driving the Coquihalla in winter means planning for mountain conditions that can change within a few kilometres: legal winter tires, a DriveBC check before you leave Hope or Merritt, a full tank and warm gear, and extra time for chain-ups and closures. Commercial drivers also need to carry the right chains and follow the chain-up and lane-restriction signs on the climb toward the summit.

The Coquihalla (Highway 5) between Hope and Merritt is the main route from the Lower Mainland into BC's Interior, and in winter it asks a lot of drivers. It's the highway many Canadians first saw on Discovery's [*Highway Thru Hell*](https://www.jamiedavistowing.com/about/), which followed Jamie Davis's heavy recovery operation, and it's still the corridor our Hope crews work every winter. Driving the Coquihalla in winter is routine for plenty of drivers, but the ones who have the easiest trips are the ones who plan for the mountain rather than the weather in town. This guide covers how risky the route really is, the sections drivers and truckers should know, what to check before you go, the rules for commercial vehicles, and what happens when a truck jackknifes or a car spins out.

## Is the Coquihalla Dangerous in Winter?

The Coquihalla is a well-maintained highway, but driving the Coquihalla in winter means crossing a mountain pass, and that changes the risk once snow starts falling.

Conditions can shift quickly between the valley at Hope and the higher ground near the Coquihalla Summit. Rain in Hope can turn into heavy snow or freezing rain partway up the climb, and visibility can drop fast in blowing snow. The Ministry even runs a dedicated Coquihalla Snowshed Protocol, a 10-step plan for keeping the highway safe during heavy snowfall.

Closures happen too. The Ministry closes sections between Hope and Merritt for planned avalanche control at the Coquihalla Summit after heavy snowfall, and collisions or spun-out trucks can stop traffic until the scene is cleared. Much of the risk is in the driver's hands: preparation, speed and following distance make the biggest difference.

## The Coquihalla Sections Drivers and Truckers Should Know

Heading north from Hope, most of the winter trouble spots sit on the long climb toward the summit.

- **Box Canyon chain-up area:** about 32 kilometres north of Hope, this is where commercial vehicles pull off to install chains before the climb. The Province expanded it in 2018 so it can hold more than 70 commercial vehicles at once, and it's for chain-up only, with no overnight parking.
- **Snowshed Hill:** the long, steep grade above Box Canyon is where loaded trucks can lose traction in snow. The Province restricts commercial vehicles from the far-left lane here so plows and emergency vehicles always have a way through, which also shortens the time it takes to reopen after a full closure.
- **Great Bear Snowshed:** the covered section of highway on the climb. DriveBC has cameras here, and they're one of the quickest ways to see what the road looks like before you reach it.
- **Coquihalla Summit:** the high point of the route and the location of the chain-off area for trucks heading north, as well as the area where avalanche control work takes place.

Heading south into Hope, the same grades become long descents, which is where speed, gear choice and brake temperature matter most for heavy vehicles. Chain-up areas, lane restrictions and signage are set by the Ministry and can change, so always follow what's posted on the day.

## Before You Leave Hope or Merritt: A Winter Checklist

A few minutes of planning before driving the Coquihalla in winter can save you hours on the highway.

- **Check DriveBC before you go.** Look at [DriveBC](https://www.drivebc.ca/) for current conditions, closures and the highway cameras, or call its 24/7 automated line at 1-800-550-4997.
- **Make sure your tires are legal and in good shape.** BC requires winter tires with the M+S or mountain/snowflake symbol and at least 3.5 mm of tread on signed highways in winter. Chains on summer tires don't count as a substitute, according to the Province.
- **Fill the tank before the climb.** A closure or a long lineup behind a recovery can leave you idling for a long time, and running out of fuel on a mountain pass in winter turns a delay into an emergency.
- **Pack for a longer wait than you expect.** Warm layers, food, water, a shovel, traction material and a charged phone or battery pack make a closure uncomfortable instead of dangerous.

## What Truckers Need to Know About Chains and Lanes on the Coquihalla

Commercial drivers have extra rules to follow on this corridor.

- **Carry the right chains.** Under the Province's [commercial vehicle tire and chain requirements](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/transportation/driving-and-cycling/traveller-information/seasonal/winter-driving/commercial), vehicles with a licensed gross vehicle weight of 11,794 kg or more must carry steel link chains, and trucks between 5,000 and 11,794 kg must carry chains or another acceptable traction device unless they're on winter-rated tires.
- **Chain up where you're told, and do it in the chain-up area.** Installing chains at Box Canyon, off the travel lanes, is safer and faster than trying to do it on the shoulder halfway up Snowshed Hill once you've already lost traction.
- **Know your configuration before the season.** Where chains go depends on your vehicle's weight, type and setup. The Province publishes chain-up guidance for common configurations, so review it with your drivers before the first storm.
- **Stay out of the left lane on Snowshed Hill.** The restriction keeps a lane open for plows and emergency vehicles.
- **Expect enforcement.** The Province has tightened commercial chain-up rules in recent years, including higher fines for trucks that don't carry chains or don't chain up when required.

As the Province puts it, it's the operator's responsibility to understand the conditions on the roads they travel and prepare their vehicle for them.

## Jackknives, Spin-Outs and What a Heavy Recovery Involves

When a loaded truck loses traction on a mountain grade, the recovery is a much bigger job than towing a car.

A jackknife happens when the trailer swings out and folds toward the tractor, often when the drive wheels lose grip or the brakes lock on a slippery grade. A spin-out on the climb can leave a truck sideways across one or more lanes. Either way, the scene needs to be made safe first. Police and highway crews manage traffic, and if anyone is hurt or a vehicle is blocking live lanes, that starts with a 911 call.

From there, the [heavy duty recovery crew](https://www.jamiedavistowing.com/heavy-duty-towing-services/) assesses the load, the ground and the weather before anything is moved. Depending on the scene, the job can involve winching the unit back into line, uprighting a rollover, correcting a shifted load or using a crane to lift equipment. Sometimes cargo has to be transferred before the truck can be moved at all. That work takes time when it's done safely, which is why lanes can stay closed even after the truck looks ready to go.

For fleet managers and dispatchers, the most useful details to give our dispatch are the unit's location and direction of travel, the load type, any hazardous materials on board and the truck and trailer configuration. Our [commercial towing team](https://www.jamiedavistowing.com/commercial-towing-services/) can also arrange transport once the unit is recovered, including [long-haul towing](https://www.jamiedavistowing.com/long-haul-international-towing/) back to your home shop.

## If You Get Stuck or Break Down on the Coquihalla

A few habits make a roadside wait on the Coquihalla safer and shorter.

Call 911 first if there's a collision, an injury or a vehicle blocking a travel lane. For a mechanical breakdown or a car stuck in the snow, call a towing company directly. If you're stopped near traffic, keep your seatbelt on and stay in the vehicle with your hazard lights on. If you run the engine for heat, make sure the tailpipe is clear of snow and open a window slightly, because a blocked exhaust can fill the cabin with carbon monoxide.

When you call, give your direction of travel and the nearest landmark, such as Box Canyon, the snowshed or the summit. For flat tires, boosts and winching out of the snow, our [roadside assistance](https://www.jamiedavistowing.com/roadside-assistance/) crews can often get you moving without a tow.

## The Coquihalla in Winter: Frequently Asked Questions

**Is the Coquihalla dangerous in winter?**
It's a well-maintained highway, but it's a mountain pass, so heavy snow, freezing rain and poor visibility can arrive quickly on the climb to the Coquihalla Summit. Legal winter tires, a DriveBC check before you leave, a slower pace and more following distance all lower the risk.

**Do you need winter tires to drive the Coquihalla?**
Yes. Winter tires are required on signed BC highways in winter, and the Coquihalla is signed. A legal winter tire carries the M+S or mountain/snowflake symbol and has at least 3.5 mm of tread, and the Province says chains on summer tires don't count as a substitute.

**Where is the chain-up area on the Coquihalla?**
The main northbound chain-up area for commercial vehicles is at Box Canyon, about 32 kilometres north of Hope, before the climb up Snowshed Hill. There's a chain-off area at the Coquihalla Summit.

**How do I check Coquihalla road conditions?**
Use DriveBC at drivebc.ca for current conditions, closures and highway cameras, including the cameras at the Great Bear Snowshed, or call the 24/7 automated line at 1-800-550-4997.

**What happens if the Coquihalla closes?**
Closures for collisions or avalanche control can stop northbound traffic at Hope and southbound traffic at Merritt until the highway reopens. The alternate routes are Highway 1 through the Fraser Canyon and Highway 3 over the Hope-Princeton, which are also mountain routes in winter, so check DriveBC before switching.
